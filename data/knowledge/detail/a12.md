## Abstract
Surrogate-basedBayesianoptimizationhasbeenwidelyappliedindesignoptimization
to increase sampling efﬁciency. However, the cost for each evaluation of the objective
function can still be very high when physical experiments or large-scale simulations are
involved. Multi-ﬁdelity Bayesian optimization is the new approach to further improve
the sampling efﬁciency by reducing the number of expensive samples at the highest
ﬁdelity level and supplementing them with less expensive ones at low-ﬁdelity levels.
In this paper, a new consequential improvement (CI) acquisition function is proposed
to allow for the simultaneous selection of the solution and the ﬁdelity level in problems
with a known hierarchy of ﬁdelity levels. The new CI acquisition function incorporates the consequential effectiveness of objective improvement with the considerations
of cost, accuracy, and validity differences between high- and low-ﬁdelity samples in
engineering practice. The new method of multi-ﬁdelity Bayesian optimization based
on the CI is demonstrated with several analytical and simulation-based design examples. In the simulation-based design optimization example, the results show that the
CI acquisition function has a decisive advantage in the sampling efﬁciency over the
other methods of multi-ﬁdelity Bayesian optimization with simultaneous selection.

## 1 Introduction

In the iterative engineering design optimization process, the objective function needs
to be evaluated at sample design solutions. When the number of design variables is
large and the dimension of the feasible solution space is high, many samples are usually
required before the optimal solution is obtained. Therefore, increasing the sampling
efﬁciency is important for design optimization. Surrogate-based optimization methods
such as Bayesian optimization (BO) (Shahriari et al. 2016) can increase the sampling
efﬁciency. In surrogate-based optimization, a surrogate of the objective function is
constructed and iteratively updated based on new samples. The surrogate predicts
the objective value of unsampled design solutions. Speciﬁcally in BO, the surrogate,
typically Gaussian process regression model, is used to identify the most promising
sample for the next iteration based on an acquisition function. In each iteration, the
solution that maximizes the acquisition function is selected as the next sample to
be evaluated. The sampling efﬁciency of surrogate-based optimization is known to be
higher than those of traditional population-based optimization methods such as genetic
algorithms and particle swarm optimization. Hybrid surrogate-assisted evolutionary
optimization methods have also been proposed to improve the sampling efﬁciency by
introducing surrogates into population-based optimization (Liu et al. 2024). Although the surrogate-based approach improves the sampling efﬁciency through
the guidance of the surrogate, the cost for each evaluation of the objective function
can still be very high, especially when physical experiments or large-scale simulations
are involved. Recently, the multi-ﬁdelity approach has attracted researchers’ attention.
In multi-ﬁdelity Bayesian optimization (MFBO), samples at low-ﬁdelity (LF) levels
are used to supplement expensive samples at the highest-ﬁdelity (HF) level. Among
ﬁdelity levels, evaluations of the objective differ in expense and accuracy. Evaluations
at the LF levels are less expensive but also less accurate. In engineering design, the most trustworthy technique to evaluate a design is usually the physical experiment or prototype which would be regarded as a HF sample
and eventually needed for validation. That is, the unique role of the HF samples in
engineering design is that they are also used for the purpose of validation ultimately.
No further validation is needed if an HF sample is chosen as the ﬁnal optimal design
after the iterative search. In contrast, when an LF sample is selected as the optimum,
it is implied that a corresponding HF level sample is still needed as the validation
of the design, which is the hidden and deferred cost associated with the LF sample.
When the multi-ﬁdelity method is used to combine experimental and simulation data
to perform design, even though simulation-based design can substantially reduce the
cost, experiments as the HF-level evaluations are still required in engineering practice
to validate the optimum and ﬁnalize the design. When the multi-ﬁdelity method is
used with simulations only, the prediction from the highest ﬁdelity and most accurate

However, the LF evaluations themselves cannot be trusted as the most reliable values.
Therefore, at least one sample at the HF level is necessary to quantify and materialize

possible with trustworthy evaluations at the HF level. Therefore, in the multi-ﬁdelity
approach for design optimization, the unique merit of HF evaluations for the validated
and materializable design improvement needs to be considered during the sample
selection. The primary contribution of this paper is a novel consequential improvement (CI)
acquisition function for MFBO, which uniquely addresses the limitations of existing
MFBO methods by simultaneously selecting the sample solution and ﬁdelity level
while accounting for the consequential effectiveness of objective improvement and
hidden cost when LF samples are selected. Different from prior MFBO approaches,
such as those with pre-scheduled ﬁdelity selections (Khatouri et al. 2020; Perdikaris
and Karniadakis 2016) or with disjointed selections of solutions and ﬁdelity levels (Shu
et al. 2021; Tran et al. 2020b), the CI acquisition function enables the simultaneous
selection of the solution and ﬁdelity level. At the same time, the new concept of hidden
and deferred cost of validation associated with LF samples is introduced in this paper.
The proposed CI acquisition function explicitly differentiates the roles of HF and
LF samples in engineering design. In the existing MFBO approaches including the
simultaneous selection methods (Poloczek et al. 2017; Takeno et al. 2020), the roles
of LF and HF samples in engineering design are treated similarly and the critical
role of validation for HF samples is omitted. In contrast, the proposed CI acquisition
function incorporates the hidden cost of validation when LF samples are selected,
since the actual improvement can only be proven through HF evaluations. As a result,
this approach prevents over-sampling at LF levels and ensures that the ﬁnal optimal
design is validated at the HF level, aligning with the common engineering practice.
By synergistically maximizing the CI acquisition function, our method achieves the
overall high sampling efﬁciency, as demonstrated through analytical and simulationbased design examples. The proposed CI acquisition function incorporates the consequential effectiveness
of objective improvement, while the solution and the ﬁdelity level of sampling are
selected simultaneously. For a selection at the HF level, the value of the acquisition
function is the quotient of the anticipated improvement in the objective and the evaluation cost over the current or immediate iteration. For a selection at an LF level, the
value of the acquisition function is the quotient of the anticipated objective improvement and the overall evaluation cost over both the current and subsequent iterations.
Therefore, the effects of LF samples and the associated ‘hidden cost’ for the required
validation are further differentiated from those of HL samples. The ‘hidden cost’ of
ﬁnal validation plays a crucial role in engineering, which has not been considered in
existing multi-ﬁdelity optimization methods such as MES and KG. The proposed CI

## 2 Background

In this section, the most relevant work on MFBO is reviewed. In Sect. 2.1, a background
of multi-ﬁdelity surrogate modeling is introduced. In Sect. 2.2, the existing MFBO
methods with scheduled selection of the ﬁdelity level are reviewed. In Sect. 2.3, the
existing MFBO methods with a disjointed selection of the solution and the ﬁdelity
level are summarized. In Sect. 2.4, the MFBO methods with simultaneous selection
of the solution and ﬁdelity level are reviewed.

## 2.1 Multi-fidelity surrogate of objective function

Multi-ﬁdelity surrogate modeling (Gano et al. 2004, 2005) has been adopted as a
computationally efﬁcient method for design optimization. Gaussian process regression is the most used surrogate because a localized uncertainty level at each unsampled
solution can be estimated in addition to the mean estimate of the objective. At each
ﬁdelity level, some additional hyperparameters, called cross-ﬁdelity correlation coef-
ﬁcients, are introduced in the surrogate. The cross-ﬁdelity correlation coefﬁcients are
optimized to ﬁt the data obtained up to the respective ﬁdelity level. For instance, the
coefﬁcients associated with the third ﬁdelity level are only trained based on the data
from the ﬁrst, second, and third ﬁdelity levels. All cross-ﬁdelity correlation coefﬁ-
cients can be ﬁt simultaneously (Han and Görtz 2012; Han et al. 2010; Zimmermann
and Han 2010). The computational expense may be further reduced through an autoregressive model (Kennedy and O’Hagan 2000) with the coefﬁcients ﬁt in the ascending
order of ﬁdelity level. More recently, a hierarchical or recursive formulation of the
multi-ﬁdelity Gaussian process was proposed to eliminate the need for cross-ﬁdelity
correlation coefﬁcients in the bi-ﬁdelity (Han and Görtz 2012) and unlimited ﬁdelity
cases (Le Gratiet and Garnier 2014; Parussini et al. 2017). This further reduces the
computational expense of training the surrogate. In this paper, this approach is used in
constructing the Gaussian process regression models. Some non-hierarchical model
fusion approaches (Chen et al. 2017; Foumani et al. 2023) were also proposed.

2.2 MFBO with scheduled selection of the fidelity level of sampling

In some methods of MFBO, the ﬁdelity level of each sample is not selected based
on the acquisition function. In the method developed by (Rezende et al. 2021), the
ﬁdelity level of sampling is predetermined, with a batch of samples taken at an LF
level alongside one sample at the HF level. The selection of a batch of samples collectively spanning the ﬁdelity levels has also been used in the work of Honarmandi et al.
(Honarmandi et al. 2022). In other methods (Khatouri et al. 2020; Nachar et al. 2020;
Perdikaris and Karniadakis 2016), the ﬁdelity level is ﬁxed between subsequent iterations. Alternatively, in the method developed by (Song et al. 2019), the sampling is
divided into a phase of exploration prior to an iterative phase of exploitation, in which
sampling is limited to the HF level. Similarly, in the method developed by (Frey et al.
2022), new samples are limited to the HF level. Because of the inﬂexibility in selecting
the ﬁdelity level of each sample, these methods provide only a limited advantage in
sampling efﬁciency.

2.3 MFBO with disjointed selection of the fidelity level of sampling

The sampling efﬁciency of MFBO can be increased by selecting both the solution and
the ﬁdelity level of sampling in each iteration. In the methods of disjointed selection,
the solution is selected before selecting the ﬁdelity level at which the solution is
sampled. In the methods of (Zhang et al. 2019) and (Korondi et al. 2021), the solution
is selected to maximize the anticipated improvement of the objective at an LF level
prior to the selection of the ﬁdelity level. Due to the discrepancy between evaluations
of the objective at different ﬁdelity levels, these methods of selecting the solution
could lead to redundant sampling. The issue of redundant sampling is resolved in the following methods, where solutions are selected to maximize various metrics related to the anticipated improvement
of the objective value at the HF level. For example, the ﬁdelity level associated with
the lowest cost can be selected from the subset of ﬁdelity levels that meet a speciﬁed
uncertainty threshold at the selected solution (Kandasamy et al. 2016, 2019, 2017;
Sorourifar et al. 2023). In the methods developed by (Tran et al. 2020a, 2020b, 2020),
the ﬁdelity level is selected to maximize the ratio between the anticipated reduction in
the uncertainty of the surrogate at the HF level and the cost of evaluation at the respective ﬁdelity level. In the method developed by (Shu et al. 2021), the ﬁdelity level is
selected to maximize a metric that is speciﬁc to each ﬁdelity level. In this method, the
anticipated improvement quantiﬁes the beneﬁt of sampling at the HF level. The beneﬁt
of an LF sample is quantiﬁed as the difference between the anticipated improvement
at the HF level after the same solution is evaluated at the current LF level and the
anticipated improvement when directly evaluated at the HF level. In the above methods, better combinations of solutions and ﬁdelity levels may be
missed during the disjointed selection because the metrics of selecting the ﬁdelity level
are speciﬁc to the selected solution. Therefore, direct extensions of these acquisition
functions for simultaneous selection do not provide the best outcome.

2.4 MFBO with simultaneous selection of the fidelity level of sampling

The sampling efﬁciency of MFBO can be further increased by selecting both the
solution and ﬁdelity level simultaneously, rather than sequentially, in each iteration.
The acquisition function in the methods of simultaneous selection is deﬁned as a
function of both sample location and ﬁdelity level. In some methods (Belakaria et al. 2020; Li et al. 2021, 2020; Takeno et al. 2020),
the simultaneous selection is done by maximizing the ratio between a multi-ﬁdelity
formulation of max-value (output-space) entropy search (MES) and the evaluation
cost. MES (Hoffman and Ghahramani 2015; Wang and Jegelka 2017) quantiﬁes the
anticipated reduction in the uncertainty associated with the optimum value of the
surrogate at the HF level. In other methods (Imani and Ghoreishi 2022; Imani et al.
2019; Poloczek et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2020), the simultaneous selection is done by
maximizing the ratio between a multi-ﬁdelity formulation of the knowledge gradient
(KG) and the evaluation cost. The KG (Frazier et al. 2009) quantiﬁes the anticipated
improvement in the optimum value of the surrogate at the HF level. However, since
the surrogate optimum is used in place of the optimum objective value, the multi-
ﬁdelity extensions of the MES and KG do not account for the discrepancy between
the surrogate and the original objective. Some additional evaluation cost is required
to verify the objective at the surrogate optimum. Due to the mismatch in the ratio
of beneﬁt to cost, multi-ﬁdelity extensions of the MES and KG could lead to oversampling at LF levels. The mismatch is due to the overly similar treatment of the
HF level versus the LF levels. An important feature of the multi-ﬁdelity strategy for
engineering design is that the objective improvement by an evaluation at a LF level
cannot be validated. The actual improvement with validation is only possible at the
HF level. In this paper, a new acquisition function, called consequential improvement, is
proposed to target the actual and validated improvement. When an LF sample is
chosen, there is a hidden cost of validation, which has not been considered in the
existing MFBO methods.

## 3.2 Proposed CI acquisition function

In this section, the new MFBO methodology with the proposed CI acquisition function is described. The existing MFBO methods only consider the immediate evaluation
costs and overlook the additional HF evaluation required to validate an LF sample’s
improvement in engineering design. In contrast, the CI acquisition function incorporates the hidden and deferred cost of validation, where the function value for a
LF selection as the quotient of the anticipated objective improvement and the overall evaluation cost, including both the current LF evaluation and the subsequent HF
evaluation for validation. For a HF selection, the acquisition function is deﬁned only
with the current iteration’s cost and improvement. This differentiation ensures that
the cost–beneﬁt analysis reﬂects the practical need for HF validation, which prevents
over-sampling at LF levels and prioritizes validated improvements. This approach
is signiﬁcant because it aligns with engineering requirements for trustworthy and
validated designs. At the same time, the CI acquisition function enhances sampling
efﬁciency, particularly in high-dimensional or high-cost scenarios, as shown in our
analytical and simulation-based examples in Sect. 4.

## 3.2.4 Proposed CI acquisition function

Instead of the above two options in 3.2.3, the proposed CI acquisition function is.

With the CI acquisition function, the subsequent iteration is only considered for the
potential selections at LF levels. For each ﬁdelity level, the CI acquisition function
is equal to the ratio between improvement and cost over the iterations for which the
consequential effects are approximated. The improvement in the current iteration is
emphasized by using separate ratios for each ﬁdelity level. This is done to reduce
the likelihood that all selections are taken at LF levels. For a potential LF selection,
the value of the proposed CI acquisition function is the quotient of the anticipated
improvement and evaluation cost for both the current selection and the presumed
subsequent selection. The relationship between the cost and beneﬁt associated with selections in the CI
acquisition function is illustrated in Fig. 1. The beneﬁt, which is quantiﬁed as the
consequential improvement, is depicted along the vertical axis. The evaluation cost
required to obtain the improvement is depicted on the horizontal axis. For sample
A at the HF level, the evaluation cost and consequential improvement are calculated
directly based on the current iteration. In contrast, for sample B at a LF level, there is
no improvement in the current iteration. Therefore, the consequential improvement of
sample B is calculated by considering the subsequent iteration which is indicated as
sample B’. For sample B’, the consequential improvement in the subsequent iteration
is associated with the evaluation cost accrued over both the current and subsequent

## 4.1 Setup of the MFBO for demonstrations

Here, the CI acquisition function is compared with the multi-ﬁdelity extensions of
the KG and the MES acquisition functions for simultaneous selection. With every
optimization example in this paper, the goal is to minimize the objective function at
the HF level as in Eq. (1). In each example, there are two initial samples at each ﬁdelity
level. The MFBO algorithms used in the multi-ﬁdelity extensions of the KG and the
MES acquisition functions are similar to the ones in Algorithm 1. In each iteration of
MFBO, x∗and a∗are selected from domains 1 and 2, respectively, to maximize
the given acquisition function, which is either the CI, the multi-ﬁdelity extension of
the KG, or the multi-ﬁdelity extension of the MES. In the examples, a maximum of 40
iterations are allowed regardless of the sampling cost. The number of initial samples
and number of iterations are chosen to allow us to analyze the performance of the new
acquisition function. The small number of initial samples matches the lowest possible
number of samples to construct a surrogate in real-world applications. The maximum
number of iterations is beyond what is usually feasible in engineering design, where
each trusted evaluation or physical experiment incurs a high cost. For real-world
engineering design applications, the HF level is the one that could be trusted to be
used as the validation of the results from other LF evaluations. For demonstration, the
HF evaluations in the analytical and engineering design examples in this paper are
regarded as ground truth. The new MFBO method is implemented in Python. The optimizations of the acquisition functions are based on the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno algorithm in
the SciPy library (Virtanen et al. 2020). The sequential least squares constrained programming algorithm (Kraft 1988) is used when equality and inequality constraints are
involved. Additionally, the normalized similarity metric

is applied to the i-th component in x, where Bi is the range of values possible for the
i-th dimension of the solutions. To further reduce the computational expense of hyperparameter ﬁtting, each hyperparameter θa associated with the same ﬁdelity level a is
set to the same value (Wang 2021). This method reduces the computational expense
of hyperparameter ﬁtting. Thus, the hyperparameters only depend on the ﬁdelity level
and are independent of solution components. Only two potential evaluations are used
when evaluating the acquisition functions based on the subsequent iteration. Additionally, hyperparameters are not updated during the temporary updates of the surrogate.
These procedures are done to reduce the computational expense of evaluating the
acquisition functions. The maximum effective expenditure of 60 was selected to demonstrate and represent a typical resource-constrained simulation-based engineering design environment

## 4.3.4 Comparison of conventional optimization methods

To contextualize the performance of the proposed CI-based MFBO methodology, a
comparative study was conducted against two well-established, conventional optimization algorithms: a gradient-based Quasi-Newton method and a population-based
Genetic Algorithm (GA). These methods were chosen to represent two fundamentally
different optimization philosophies: the Quasi-Newton method exempliﬁes a "whitebox" local optimization strategy that leverages detailed gradient information, while
the GA represents a "black-box" global search metaheuristic that operates without
such information. Both the Quasi-Newton and GA methods were implemented in the MATLAB
environment using the highly-regarded fmincon and ga solvers from the Optimization (The MathWorks 2025b) and Global Optimization (The MathWorks 2025a)
Toolboxes, respectively. The use of these standardized tools ensures a robust and
reproducible benchmark. To enable the efﬁcient application of the gradient-based
method, an analytical sensitivity analysis was implemented within the Finite Element
Method (FEM) solver to compute the exact gradients of the objective and constraint
functions. The accuracy of these analytical gradients was veriﬁed against numerical
ﬁnite-differencing to ensure the reliable performance of the Quasi-Newton algorithm.
The source codes for these implementations have been made publicly available on
MATLAB Central File Exchange for transparency and future research (Aydogdu
2025a, b). The additional feasibility and performance reports for these benchmark
runs are provided in the Supplementary Material. A critical aspect of this comparison was maintaining experimental fairness. As
the true bottleneck in complex engineering design is the computational cost of each
function evaluation (i.e., each FEM simulation), a strict budget was imposed. The
maximum number of function evaluations was set to 40 for both methods, directly
corresponding to the "Effective expenditure" budget used for the MFBO methods. For
the GA, this budget was implemented with a population size of 4 over 10 generations.
To ensure a direct and unambiguous comparison, the CI-MFBO results used in this
section correspond to the single high-ﬁdelity case (utilizing all three ﬁdelity levels), as
both the Quasi-Newton and GA methods operate on the fully constrained, high-ﬁdelity
problem deﬁnition.

## 5 Concluding remarks

In this paper, a new CI acquisition function is proposed to further increase the sampling
efﬁciency of MFBO. With the new CI acquisition function, the sample solution and
the ﬁdelity level of the objective evaluation are selected simultaneously. Simultaneous
selection is done to synergistically maximize the proposed CI acquisition function. In
engineering design, the actual improvement needs to be validated with samples at the
HF level, which usually involves physical experiments. The proposed CI acquisition

123

<!-- page: 35 -->
Consequential improvement acquisition function for efﬁcient
2783

function incorporates the consequential and actual improvement of the design objective. This implies that the ﬁnal selection will always be at the HF level in applications.
It also avoids the situation that samples are only taken at LF levels. The proposed CI acquisition function was applied to analytical functions and
simulation-based design optimization examples. The sampling efﬁciency of the CI
acquisition function is compared to those of the other MFBO methods for simultaneous selection, including the multi-ﬁdelity extensions of the KG and MES. The
sensitivity of the sampling efﬁciency to different evaluation cost ratios is analyzed.
Finally, a multiscale materials and device design example is developed to assess the
sampling efﬁciency in a practical engineering design situation, where the computational effort of surrogate construction is justiﬁed by the substantial cost savings from
the expensive simulations. In the multiscale design example, a multi-ﬁdelity extension
of a 25-bar spatial truss is solved. For simple analytical examples, the results indicate
that the sampling efﬁciency depends on the evaluation cost ratios. For the simulationbased design examples where the true evaluation costs are considered, the results show
that the CI has a decisive advantage in the sampling efﬁciency over the other MFBO
methods for simultaneous selection. One limitation of MFBO is the additional computational expense required to evaluate the acquisition function due to the use of multiple surrogate models. In comparison
to the multi-ﬁdelity extensions of the KG or MES, the computational expense is
reduced for the CI acquisition function because the additional surrogate is not needed
for evaluations of potential selections at the HF level. In this paper, the computational expense of ﬁtting the surrogate was reduced by building the multi-ﬁdelity
surrogate without cross-ﬁdelity correlation coefﬁcients. The cost of surrogate ﬁtting
was further reduced by applying an equal value constraint on the hyperparameters.
Furthermore, only two potential evaluations were used when evaluating the acquisition functions based on the subsequent iteration. Additionally, hyperparameters are
not updated during the temporary updates of the surrogate. These strategies are taken
to reduce the computational expense of evaluating the CI acquisition function. However, the accuracies of the surrogate and the acquisition function evaluations are also
reduced. Reducing the accuracies of the surrogate and the acquisition function evaluations could reduce the sampling efﬁciency of MFBO. Further work on quantifying
the impact of these strategies on the sampling efﬁciency and on the computational
expense associated with the algorithm itself is needed. When the CI is applied in MFBO, the prioritization of the computational expense
versus the sampling efﬁciency can depend on the needs of the optimization problem.
While LF evaluations are less expensive, they are also less informative. Therefore, a
greater total number of samples are generally required for MFBO than for BO without
multiple ﬁdelity levels. The sampling efﬁciency of MFBO might not be improved
over that of single-ﬁdelity BO if LF evaluations do not provide enough cost savings.
Conversely, the CI method may not provide signiﬁcant advantages for simple problems of low dimensionality, when cost differences between ﬁdelity levels are small, or
low-ﬁdelity models deviate signiﬁcantly from high-ﬁdelity ones. In those cases, multi-
ﬁdelity approaches are less effective, and LF evaluations may not be cost-effective
since they are relatively inaccurate in comparison to the cost saving. Additionally,
regardless of the evaluation costs, evaluations at LF levels must still be sufﬁciently

123

<!-- page: 36 -->
2784
I. Aydogdu et al.

accurate to be useful for informing the surrogate. Wasteful selections at LF levels may
be avoided with the proposed CI acquisition function. However, the resources that
have already been expended for the initial samples at LF levels cannot be recouped.
Future work can be done to build models to predict whether any of the LF levels
are useful based on the initial samples for improving the overall cost-effectiveness.
By eliminating ineffective ﬁdelity levels, the computational expenses associated with
updating the surrogate and evaluating the acquisition function can be further reduced.
Future studies will also include how to extend the CI acquisition function for multiobjective optimization and parallel sampling. However, either of these approaches will
decrease the sampling efﬁciency compared to the single-objective BO with sequential
sampling. It is also beneﬁcial to evaluate how the CI acquisition function can handle
complex cost structures in real-world engineering scenarios, such as time-varying or
stochastic cost ratios, considerations of setup time, parallel or batch evaluations, or
uncertainty in the computational or experimental expenses. New strategies of incorporating dynamic cost models into the CI acquisition function, where costs are updated
iteratively based on runtime estimates or probabilistic distributions, may improve the
robustness of performance.
